Timing the hero

Where the clock should live

A timer in the browser decides which shop is showing right now. A timer on the server decides which shop is rank one today. Only one of those is fair to merchants and free for shoppers.

If you do time it, this is the anatomy

A ring that fills on the Next control, a bar along the bottom edge, and a visible Pause. The ring is the honest one — it shows the wait attached to the thing that will act, rather than making the whole slide feel restless.

Not optional

WCAG 2.2.2: anything that moves by itself for more than five seconds beside other content must have a way to pause, stop or hide it. An auto-advancing hero without a Pause control is an accessibility failure, not a taste question.

It must also stop under prefers-reduced-motion, and pause the moment a finger or cursor touches it.

No timer —

The hero holds one shop until someone acts. The rail beside it carries the other seven, so “there is more” is shown by layout instead of by motion. Costs nothing on a slow connection because the other photos load lazily, when scrolled to.

What the rail already does. Nothing moves, nothing is missed.
One slow pass 8s, then stops

Advances once every eight seconds, stops after it has been through the set once, and pauses on any touch. Long enough to read a shop name and decide; short enough that a bored visitor sees a second shop. Never loops back to the start.

Reasonable compromise if the hero must feel alive.
Continuous loop 5s, forever

Keeps cycling while the page is open. Visitors learn to ignore the region entirely, the first slide takes nearly all the clicks anyway, and every photo has to load up front — real money on a 2G connection for slides most people never see.

The pattern with the most evidence against it.
What I would do

Put the clock on the server. Rank one changes once a day, so every featured merchant gets a real turn in the big space and a shopper can still go back to the shop they saw this morning. Leave the hero still, and let the rail do the work of saying there are more.

A browser timer only changes who is on screen in the next eight seconds. It does not make featuring fairer, it costs bandwidth your merchants' customers pay for, and it drags a Pause control and a reduced-motion branch along behind it.
